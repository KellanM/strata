# Hopkins study: sources

Research notes on Claude C. Hopkins (1866–1932), gathered on 2026-10-30 for Strata's App Store listing and ads. This is research only: nothing here changes the listing. Quotes are exact from the primary texts and kept apart from interpretation. Anything unverified is marked.

**Key.** [V] means I fetched the source and read it, or viewed the scan. [O] means I read only the newspaper's OCR text, not the image. [U] means unverified: from memory or secondary report, not re-checked in this session.

## 2. Campaign passages from *My Life in Advertising*

### Bissell carpet sweepers (ML IV, "How I Got My Start in Advertising")
- On John E. Powers' pamphlet: "There is not one word in that pamphlet which will lead women to buy."
- To the directors who wanted to talk about mechanisms: "Why not talk broom action, patent dumping devices, cyco bearings, and the great things I have created?" "I am talking to women," I replied. "They are not mechanics. I want to talk the things which they will understand and appreciate."
- "I offered both free for Christmas, not as a gift, but as a reward. Not then, or ever since, have I asked a purchase. That is useless. I have simply offered service."
- "I offered a privilege, not an inducement. I appeared as a benefactor, not as a salesman."
- Vermilion wood: "The pamphlet told a story intended to arouse curiosity, to bring women to see that wood. No other activating factor compares with curiosity."
- Scarcity, as he wrote to dealers: "They will never be offered again."

### Schlitz (ML VII, "Medical Advertising"; also SA 7)
- "All brewers at that time were crying 'Pure.' They put the word 'Pure' in large letters. Then they took double pages to put it in larger letters. The claim made about as much impression on people as water makes on a duck."
- What he saw: "plate-glass rooms where beer was dripping over pipes … filtered air … great filters filled with white-wood pulp … How every bottle was cleaned four times by machinery … artesian wells, where they went 4,000 feet deep for pure water … their original mother yeast cell. It had been developed by 1,200 experiments" (OCR reads "i,2joo"; SA 7 gives "1,018 experiments").
- "'Why don't you tell people these things? Why do you merely try to cry louder than others that your beer is pure?'" The brewers replied: "'the processes we use are just the same as others use. No one can make good beer without them.'"
- "I told a story common to all good brewers, but a story which had never been told. I gave purity a meaning."
- The general rule: "But tell the pains you take to excel. Tell factors and features which others deem too commonplace to claim. Your product will come to typify those excellencies. If others claim them afterward, it will only serve to advertise you."
- "There are few advertised products which cannot be imitated. Few who dominate a field have any exclusive advantage. They were simply the first to tell certain convincing facts."
- **Note on "washed with live steam."** That phrase is not in Hopkins' own account. He writes "cleaned four times by machinery". "Live steam" does appear in a 1909 Schlitz ad (see §3, A3), which postdates his Racine years, and in a 1908 Van Camp's ad (A6). The popular retelling looks like a blend of the two.

### Medical advertising and the druggist's guarantee (ML VII, VIII)
- "People were not buying medicine, they were buying results."
- "One must outbid all others in some way. He must offer advantages in qualities, service, or terms, or he must create a seeming advantage by citing facts which others fail to cite. Crying a name or brand is not sufficient." (VII)
- His proprietary ads: "'Try this cough remedy; watch the benefits it brings. It cannot harm, for no opiates are in it. If it succeeds, the cough will stop. If it fails, it is free. Your own druggist signs the warrant.'" (VIII)
- "When we make an offer one cannot reasonably refuse, it is pretty sure to gain acceptance." (VIII)

### Liquozone (ML VIII, "My Liquozone Experience")
- The plan: "Let us buy the first fifty-cent bottle. Then, to all who accept, let us offer a guaranty on six dollar bottles."
- The test: "We found in those test cities that our inquiries for free bottles cost us 18 cents each. We waited thirty days, and we found that our sale was 90 cents per inquirer." Scaled up: "The average cost per request was 18 cents, just as it was in our test cities."
- "Ask a person to take a chance on you, and you have a fight. Offer to take a chance on him, and the way is easy."
- **Caution [U].** Liquozone was among the nostrums attacked in Samuel Hopkins Adams' "The Great American Fraud" (*Collier's*, 1905). I am working from memory and did not re-check it here. The ads I saw (A4, A5) claim to kill the germs of cancer and consumption. Hopkins' methods sold an unproven product just as well as a good one.

### Van Camp's pork and beans (ML IX)
- Research first: "I found that 94 per cent of the housewives baked their own pork and beans. Only 6 per cent were amenable to any canned-bean argument. Yet all the advertisers of pork and beans were merely crying, 'Buy my brand.'"
- "I started a campaign to argue against home baking. Of course I offered samples … I told of the sixteen hours required to bake beans at home … of our steam ovens where we baked beans for hours at 245 degrees" (OCR reads "145"; the 1908 ad, A6, reads "245 degrees").
- On substitution: "I came out with headlines, 'Try Our Rivals, Too.' … If we were certain enough of our advantage to invite such comparisons, people were certain enough to buy."
- "The greatest two faults in advertising lie in boasts and in selfishness."
- "We told just the same story that any rival could have told, but all others thought the story was too commonplace."
- Evaporated milk, New York: "As a result of that ad 1,460,000 coupons were presented … 1,460,000 homes were trying Van Camp's Milk after reading our story, and all in a single day."
- Secret gift: "Describe a gift, and some will decide that they want it, more will decide that they don't. But everybody wants a secret gift." And: "The gift must not be disappointing."
- "I have no sympathy with dignified and orthodox advertising. We are in business to get results."

### Goodyear (ML XI, "Tire Advertising")
- "They told me the straight-side would not rim-cut. And that type of construction had, size for size, 10 per cent greater air capacity. 'Then why,' I asked, 'don't you emphasize those results? Results are what men are after. They care not how you get them.'"
- "He tells of the things he takes pride in — his methods and processes, the size of his plant, the age of his business, etc. The advertising man must study the consumer and tell what he wants to know."
- "I coined the name 'No-Rim-Cut Tires.' Across every ad we ran the heading, 'No-Rim-Cut Tires, 10% Oversize.'"
- "People follow the crowds. It is hard for them in most things to analyze reasons and worth, so they accept the verdict of the majority."
- "There is a great advantage in a name that tells a story … the right name may form a reasonably complete ad which all who run may read."
- Losing the account: "There developed a desire for institutional advertising which I never could approve … boasting is the last thing people want to hear."

### Palmolive (ML XII, "Early History of Palmolive")
- Started as a $700 test in Benton Harbor. "Above the ads in a box we announced that in a few days we would buy a cake of Palmolive for every woman who applied. That offer multiplied the readers of our ads."
- "'We Will Buy' is a much better headline than '10-Cent Cake Free.'" And: "The 'free' offer cheapens a product."
- "I have never found that it paid to give either a sample or a full-size package to people who do not request it."
- "The repeat sales in Benton Harbor paid for the advertising before the bills were due."
- "The simple things, easily understood, striking a popular chord, are the appeals which succeed with the masses. They often sound to the intellectual like excerpts from Mother Goose. Dutch Cleanser chases dirt, Ivory Soap floats … Keep Your Schoolgirl Complexion — such things win the nine-tenths." (He gives these as examples. He does not claim to have written them; see §4.)
- Shaving cream: "I sent out some research men to interview men by the hundreds. I asked them what they most desired in a shaving cream." The chemist then proved figures (lather "250 times": the ML OCR reads "150", but SA 7 reads 250, and this OCR often misreads a 2 as a 1; beard softened "within one minute", "ten minutes on the face"). "But we were the first to give figures on results. And one actual figure counts for more than countless platitudes."
- Shampoo failed: "The appeal presented was, 'Buy my brand instead of the other fellow's,' and such appeals never go far."

### Puffed Wheat and Puffed Rice (ML XIII; SA 6, 9)
- "I watched the process, where the grains were shot from guns. And I coined the phrase, 'Foods shot from guns.'" A rival called it "the theory of an imbecile". "But that theory proved attractive. It aroused curiosity."
- "First, I established a personality — Professor A. P. Anderson … Make a man famous and you make his creation famous."
- "Then in every ad I pictured these grains eight times normal size. I made people want to see them … In every grain we created 125,000,000 steam explosions" (OCR "115,000,000"; SA 6 reads "125 million").
- His mistakes: newspapers reached people who could not afford the product. "Then we distributed millions of samples promiscuously. The samples themselves did not win many users." Half-price offers: "It is just as hard to sell at a half price as at a full price to people not converted."
- Quaker Oats: "No one can profitably change habits in paid print. The advertiser comes in when those habits are changed."
- Testing: "Perhaps one time in fifty a guess may be right. But fifty times in fifty an actual test tells you what to do and avoid."

### Pepsodent (ML XIV)
- "In the middle of one book I found a reference to the mucin plaques on teeth, which I afterward called the film … I resolved to advertise this tooth paste as a creator of beauty."
- "Preventive measures were not popular. People will do anything to cure a trouble, but little to prevent it."
- "I had learned that repulsive ideas seldom won readers or converts. People do not want to read of the penalties. They want to be told of rewards."
- "I never referred to disasters. I never pictured the afflicted. Every illustration I ever used showed attractive people and beautiful teeth." (The ads I saw only half bear this out; see A10.)
- "We keyed every ad by the coupon. We tried out hundreds of ads. Week by week the results were reported to me, and with each report came the headline we employed."
- "I never even mentioned that Pepsodent was for sale. I never quoted its price. My whole apparent object was to prove at our cost what Pepsodent could do."
- "When we featured a free offer at the top of our ads we divided our results by four … It made us traders, simply seeking to sell, not scientists seeking to benefit."
- "One series of ads which I prepared would have wrecked it in three months … I caught my mistakes by the coupon — caught them quickly."
- "None of us can afford to rely on judgment or experience. We must feel our way."

### Mail order and personality (ML XV)
- A $5 article: replies cost $14.20 from a "larger, more alluring ad", and 41 cents from the plain one the advertiser had first rejected "it seemed so unattractive".
- The credit house given a woman's name: "In reality our offers were the same as those made by the people next door, but our attitude was different."
- "Say, 'Lowest prices in existence' and people ignore you … But say that you sell at 3 per cent net profit, and most people believe you."

### The rules as he summed them up (ML XVII, "Scientific Advertising")
- "Brilliant writing has no place in advertising. A unique style takes attention from the subject … In fishing for buyers, as in fishing for bass, one should not reveal the hook."
- "Use the shortest words possible. Let every phrase ring with sincerity. From start to finish offer service."
- "Frivolity has no place in advertising. Nor has humor … **This does not apply to amusement advertising, but it does to all other forms.**"
- "Appeal for money in a lightsome way and you will never get it. 'Sunny Jim' proved that, so did 'Spotless Town.' … People do not buy from clowns."
- "Never seek to amuse … Amusement is transient."
- "Cut out every word which is not worth that $10. Never repeat."
- "Good ads on any line cannot vary greatly. They must be complete, and completeness means similarity. The great difference lies in the headline."
- "The best school I know is canvassing, going from home to home."

### Misattributions and popular retellings to avoid
- **"The Beer That Made Milwaukee Famous"** was Schlitz's slogan before Hopkins. It appears on his ads but is not his.
- **"Washed with live steam"** is not in his account (see §2 Schlitz).
- **"Keep That Schoolgirl Complexion"** (Palmolive): Hopkins quotes it as an example of a simple appeal (ML XII) and doesn't claim it. Treat it as not his. [U] as to its actual author.
- **The Pepsodent "habit loop" / tingle story** (popularised by Charles Duhigg, *The Power of Habit*, 2012) is not in Hopkins' own Pepsodent chapter. His account is about the film, beauty, keyed coupons and not featuring "free". Don't cite Hopkins for the habit-loop claims. [U] as to Duhigg's sources.
- **"Reason-why" advertising** is usually credited to John E. Kennedy at Lord & Thomas, before Hopkins. [U]
- **"Ivory Soap floats"** and **"Dutch Cleanser chases dirt"** are cited by Hopkins (SA 5, ML XII), not written by him.

## 4. Mechanisms

Each entry gives (a) the claim with a quote, (b) where it shows in a real ad or campaign, (c) why it worked and when it fails, and (d) how it might transfer to Strata's 30-character subtitle, 170-character promotional text, the description's first three lines, and the screenshot captions. Strata's current lines, for reference (from `store/app-store-listing.md`): subtitle "A calm 3D puzzle game"; promo "Every stone keeps a secret. Read the numbers on its faces, chisel away what doesn't belong, and watch a small, lovely thing come out of the rock. No timers. No ads."; description opening "Every stone keeps something inside. / Strata is a quiet puzzle about finding it."

### M1. Specifics, not generalities
- **(a)** "Platitudes and generalities roll off the human understanding like water from a duck." "The weight of an argument may often be multiplied by making it specific." "One actual figure counts for more than countless platitudes." (SA 7; ML XII)
- **(b)** Puffed Grains: "eight times normal size", "sixty minutes in a heat of 550 degrees" (A7). Van Camp's: "245 degrees", "84 per cent nutriment" (A6). Goodyear: "10% Oversize" (ML XI, A9). The "3 per cent net profit" mail-order claim (ML XV).
- **(c)** A definite figure reads as a tested fact, and "people do not expect an advertiser to lie" about a figure (SA 7). It fails when the figure is untrue, or when it is true but measures nothing the reader wants ("a good universal joint", SA 5). It also fails once every rival quotes the same kind of figure.
- **(d)** Strata's "182 stones across 15 chapters" is a specific of size, which matters less to a free-game browser than a specific of experience. Concrete nouns are the game's specifics: "a lantern, a fox curled asleep, a lighthouse". So are rule facts a player can check: "twelve short lessons", "the rules never change". The subtitle "A calm 3D puzzle game" is all generality; "calm" is the "Pure" of this category. Candidates of the same length: "Carve the shape out of stone" (28), "3D nonograms carved in stone" (28). Captions should name the object on screen ("A fox, asleep inside the stone"), not adjectives.

### M2. Tell what everyone does but nobody says (the preemptive claim)
- **(a)** "I told a story common to all good brewers, but a story which had never been told. I gave purity a meaning." "Tell factors and features which others deem too commonplace to claim … If others claim them afterward, it will only serve to advertise you." (ML VII) "There is nearly always something impressive which others have not told." (SA 12)
- **(b)** Schlitz's plate-glass rooms and filtered air (A1, A2). Van Camp's "We told just the same story that any rival could have told" (ML IX; A6). Palmolive Shaving Cream's chemist figures (ML XII).
- **(c)** The first teller owns the fact, and the process makes a vague virtue believable. It fails if buyers already take the fact for granted and do not care. It also fails if the fact is inflated, or if the "process" is invented.
- **(d)** What does every good nonogram do that store pages rarely say? Possibly that each puzzle has exactly one answer and can be solved by logic alone, without guessing. **Claim this only if the level generator or checker guarantees it for all 182 stones; verify before use.** Other candidates of the same kind: every stone was shaped by hand rather than generated (if true), or every hint is a real next move. A description line could read "Every stone has one answer, and you can always reason your way to it." Done well, this is Hopkins' Schlitz move. Without a guarantee, it is his Liquozone problem.

### M3. A headline that hails the right reader
- **(a)** "The purpose of a headline is to pick out people you can interest … 'Hey there, Bill Jones' … Address the people you seek, and them only." (SA 5) "A headline is intended to salute the people you desire to reach. It is just like a bell-boy in a hotel calling for Mr. Jones." (ML XVII)
- **(b)** Pepsodent: "To the Women Who Decide the Home Tooth Paste" (A10). His keyed-headline record: "nearly two thousand headlines used on a single product" (SA 5), and "a change in headlines to multiply returns from five to ten times over".
- **(c)** It filters attention to people who can be converted, and the reader self-selects. It fails if the hailed group is too small ("one in a hundred", the asthma case in SA 10), or if a clever, blind headline draws a crowd that never converts.
- **(d)** On the App Store, the "headline" is the name, subtitle and first screenshot, read together in search results. "Nonogram" hails exactly the right reader but is jargon to everyone else; "calm" hails a mood, not a player. The current split (subtitle "3D puzzle", keywords "nonogram") is defensible. A test could pit "A calm 3D puzzle game" against "Nonograms, carved in 3D" (24). The first description line can hail directly: "If you love picture logic puzzles, this one is carved in stone." Don't name Picross or competitors in metadata. Captions can hail too: "For players who like to take their time".

### M4. Tell the full story: the reader reads once
- **(a)** "When you once get a person's attention, then is the time to accomplish all you can ever hope with him … what you fail to tell him in that ad is something he may never know." (SA 8) "Every ad should include whatever we have found appealing to any considerable class." (ML XVII)
- **(b)** Puffed Grains (A7) puts process, uses, personality, crowd and price on one page. Van Camp's (A6) covers taste, digestion, process, value and comparison in one ad.
- **(c)** The prospect decides in a single reading, and serials "almost never connect". It fails when the completeness buries the headline claim. It also fails where the decision is too small to justify length: Hopkins concedes that "One sentence may tell a complete story on a line like chewing gum" (SA 8).
- **(d)** Each surface must stand alone, because a browser may see only one of them. The subtitle should say what the game *is*. The promo text should carry the full proposition (what you do, what you get, the risk removers), not news. The first three description lines should do the same; the rest is for readers who are already interested. Each screenshot caption should be a complete claim, readable without the others, because people swipe two or three and stop. Captions shouldn't run as a serial ("1. Read… 2. Chisel…").

### M5. Length: complete, not long
- **(a)** "The more you tell the more you sell" (SA 4). Against that: "There is no fixed rule on this subject of brevity … whether long or short, an advertising story should be reasonably complete." (SA 8) "Cut out every word which is not worth that $10. Never repeat." (ML XVII)
- **(b)** The mail-order ad whose replies cost 41 cents against $14.20 for the "larger, more alluring ad" (SA 4; ML XV).
- **(c)** Length pays when a decision is costly and the reader is already interested. It fails when space is fixed and the reader is scanning.
- **(d)** A free install is a chewing-gum decision. The full story here is three facts: what you do (read numbers, carve), what you get (a hidden object, a map of 15 lands), and what it won't do to you (no ads, no timers, offline). Fit all three into 170 characters, then spend the 4,000-character description on the readers who tap "more". Take his "cut every word not worth $10" literally for the 30- and 170-character fields.

### M6. Service, not salesmanship: don't ask them to buy
- **(a)** "The best ads ask no one to buy. That is useless. Often they do not quote a price … The ads are based entirely on service." (SA 3) "I never even mentioned that Pepsodent was for sale." (ML XIV) "'Buy my brand' … never appealed to anybody, and it never will." (ML IX) "Phrases like 'Insist on this brand,' 'Avoid imitations' … indicate a motive with which buyers cannot sympathize." (ML XVII)
- **(b)** The Pepsodent coupon offers a test, not a sale (A10). The Bissell dealer letters offered "a privilege, not an inducement" (ML IV). Goodyear offered a free book of "all known ways to economize on tires" (A9).
- **(c)** Visible selling raises resistance ("reveal the hook"), and service puts the writer on the reader's side. It fails when the product has nothing to offer beyond the brand: Palmolive Shampoo (ML XII), evaporated milk as a staple (ML IX). Note that his own Van Camp's ad (A6) does ask for an order.
- **(d)** The GET button already does the asking. Copy and captions should never say "Download now", "Play today!" or "Don't miss out". They should *teach*. A caption like "Each number counts the cubes that stay" is service, and it shows the game is learnable before install. Strata's lesson card already does this, and the store page can borrow it.

### M7. Samples and trials, and to whom
- **(a)** "Give samples to interested people only. Give them only to people who exhibit that interest by some effort. Give them only to people to whom you have told your story." (SA 13) "Ask a person to take a chance on you, and you have a fight. Offer to take a chance on him, and the way is easy." (ML VIII) "The 'free' offer cheapens a product." (ML XII) "When we featured a free offer at the top of our ads we divided our results by four." (ML XIV)
- **(b)** The Liquozone free 50¢ bottle and druggist's warrant (ML VIII; A4). Palmolive's "We Will Buy" coupon after a story run (ML XII). Van Camp's 1,460,000 coupons in a day (ML IX). Pepsodent's ten-day tube, with "Free" kept out of the headline (A10).
- **(c)** A trial after a story confirms what was promised ("she expects to find the qualities you told", SA 6). It fails when samples go to the uninterested ("waifs on the doorsteps"), when "free" signals cheapness, or when a part-payment coupon sits in between ("as hard to get half price … as … the full price", SA 13).
- **(d)** A free game *is* the sample, so the lesson is not to lead with "Free". In this category, "free" reads as "full of ads and purchases", the way "free" made Pepsodent look like a trader. The working risk reversal is the druggist's warrant: "No ads. No in-app purchases. No sign-in." The current promo ends with "No ads", which is right. Hopkins' other condition matters more: tell the story *before* the trial, so the first minute in the game "confirms the qualities you claim". The first lesson should deliver the promise the first screenshot makes.

### M8. Positive, not negative
- **(a)** "Show the bright side … Don't show the wrinkles you propose to remove, but the face as it will appear … Tell people what to do, not what to avoid … the positive ad outpulls the other four to one." (SA 18) "People will do anything to cure a trouble, but little to prevent it." (ML XIV; SA 10)
- **(b)** The Pepsodent headlines and pictures sell beauty ("Note the Glitter", A10), though the body copy is plainly negative (germs, pyorrhea). Hopkins' cough-remedy line, "It cannot harm, for no opiates are in it" (ML VIII), is a negative used as reassurance, not as the appeal.
- **(c)** Readers move toward rewards, and "prevention is not a popular subject". The negative works only for people already suffering ("the afflicted").
- **(d)** This fits a calm brand. Sell what appears ("a small, lovely thing comes out of the rock"), not escape from stress or "brain decline" (a prevention appeal, and a health claim). "No timers. No ads." are negatives, but they work like "no opiates": risk removers placed after the appeal. Keep them last, as now. Captions should show the reveal, not the empty block.

### M9. Curiosity
- **(a)** "We learn, for instance, that curiosity is one of the strongest of human incentives." (SA 6) "No other activating factor compares with curiosity." (ML IV) "Everybody wants a secret gift." (ML IX)
- **(b)** "Foods shot from guns", with the grains pictured eight times size (A7; ML XIII). The vermilion wood with its convicts, elephants and the Ganges (ML IV). Van Camp's secret gift (ML IX). In each case the curiosity points straight at the product.
- **(c)** Curiosity pulls readers into the copy and makes them want to *see* the thing. It fails when it is a "blind headline" unrelated to the product (SA 5), or when the reveal disappoints ("The gift must not be disappointing").
- **(d)** Strata's core loop is a curiosity mechanism: something is hidden in the stone. "Every stone keeps a secret" is already the shot-from-guns move. A strong screenshot sequence is a half-carved stone captioned "What's inside?", then the finished object. Promo text can open on the question. Hopkins' condition is that the reveal must be worth it: lead with the best objects (the dragon, the lighthouse), not a cube.

### M10. Individuality: a personality and a name that tells
- **(a)** "Whenever possible we introduce a personality into our ads. By making a man famous we make his product famous." "Then we take care not to change an individuality which has proved appealing." (SA 17) "There is great advantage in a name that tells a story." "Another serious fault in coined names is frivolity." (SA 20)
- **(b)** Prof. A. P. Anderson (A7; ML XIII), Howard Coffin for Chalmers (ML X), the motherly signed letters for women's credit (SA 6; ML XV), Edna Wallace Hopper (ML XVIII). Names that tell: "No-Rim-Cut", "All-Weather" (ML XI), "Puffed" (ML XIII).
- **(c)** People trust a person over a "soulless corporation", and a consistent voice builds acquaintance. It fails if the persona is fabricated or the voice keeps changing ("a change in our characteristics would compel our best friends to get acquainted all over").
- **(d)** "Strata: Stone Puzzles" half tells its story. An indie maker's plain, signed line in the description would be his personality move, if it is true and Kellan wants it ("I made Strata for quiet evenings…"). The bigger lesson is not to change the voice. Store page, ads, lesson cards and site should sound like one person: same words for the same things (which the copy study already flagged), and the same calm.

### M11. Testing with keyed coupons
- **(a)** "We learn the principles and prove them by repeated tests. This is done through keyed advertising, by traced returns, largely by the use of coupons." "Our final conclusions are always based on cost per customer." (SA 1) "Now we let the thousands decide what the millions will do." (SA 15) "I caught my mistakes by the coupon — caught them quickly." (ML XIV)
- **(b)** Pepsodent's weekly headline reports (ML XIV). Liquozone's 18¢ inquiry against 90¢ sale in test towns (ML VIII). Palmolive's $700 Benton Harbor test (ML XII). The Quaker Two-minute/Quick Quaker votes (ML XIII).
- **(c)** Judgement is usually wrong ("often nine times in ten", SA 4), and measured cost per customer settles arguments. It fails without volume. It also fails when the key measures the wrong thing: "One ad may bring too many worthless replies" (SA 1).
- **(d)** App Store Connect has the coupons. Custom Product Pages give each ad its own keyed landing page, and Product Page Optimization tests icons and screenshots. [U] As I recall, PPO does not test subtitle or description text, and promotional text can change without review; check both. Small traffic means slow tests, so test big differences (the curiosity-led first screenshot against a rules-led one), not commas. Key on retained players (day-7, or chapter 2 reached), not installs. That is his "cost per customer", not "cost per reply".

### M12. People are not amused: no frivolity, no fine writing
- **(a)** "Fine writing is a distinct disadvantage. So is unique literary style. They take attention from the subject." (SA 2) "Don't try to be amusing. Money spending is a serious matter." (SA 2) "People do not buy from clowns … This does not apply to amusement advertising." (ML XVII)
- **(b)** He names the failures: "Sunny Jim" and "Spotless Town" (ML XVII). The plain mail-order ad beat the "alluring" one (ML XV).
- **(c)** Wit and style pull attention to the writer and away from the product, and invite distrust ("fear of over-influence"). By his own exception, the rule weakens for products that *are* amusement.
- **(d)** A game is amusement, so the "no humour" rule mostly doesn't apply. The "no fine writing" rule does. "Every stone keeps a secret" works because it is also a plain description of the mechanic. A line that is only lovely ("a small, lovely thing come out of the rock") should be tested against a plain one ("and watch a fox, a lantern or a dragon appear"). Strata's calm voice and Hopkins agree on "Use the shortest words possible. Let every phrase ring with sincerity."

### M13. Don't try to change habits; go to the converted
- **(a)** "Changing people's habits is very expensive." (SA 10) "No one can profitably change habits in paid print. The advertiser comes in when those habits are changed." (ML XIII)
- **(b)** The Quaker Oats educational campaign failed (ML XIII). Toothbrush converts cost $20–25 each (SA 10). Van Camp's was the exception: he argued against home baking only after research showed the 94 per cent (ML IX).
- **(c)** Educating a market benefits every rival, and converts cost more than they return. It fails as a rule when there is no existing habit to ride. Then the habit must be borrowed from a neighbouring one, which is what Van Camp's did with downtown lunch beans.
- **(d)** Don't spend store space teaching "what is a nonogram" to people who have never solved one. Aim at people who already play logic and picture puzzles (keywords, ad targeting, the hail in M3), and make the 3D twist the news. For everyone else, borrow a neighbouring habit: "If you like jigsaws, try carving one."

### M14. Follow the crowd, with real numbers
- **(a)** "People follow the crowds … they accept the verdict of the majority." (ML XI) "Invite them to follow the crowd." (SA 18)
- **(b)** "Goodyear tire sales trebled last year" (A9), "1,000,000 Dishes Daily" (A7), "Over One Million Barrels" (A1), and Van Camp's count of lunch counters serving its beans (ML IX).
- **(c)** It saves the reader judging merit. It fails when the numbers are small, invented or unverifiable.
- **(d)** At launch there is no crowd, and none should be implied. Later, ratings and true counts ("a million stones carved") can do this. Until then, the crowd lever is off.

### M15. Pictures that sell, not decorate
- **(a)** "Use pictures only when they form a better selling argument than the same amount of space set in type." "In the advertising of Puffed Grains the pictures of the grains were found to be most effective. They awake curiosity." (SA 9) The incubator ad's chicken silhouettes added "50 percent" to the cost per reply and "not one added sale" (SA 4).
- **(b)** The Puffed Grains page (A7) is half grain picture. The Pepsodent picture shows the result, a smile (A10).
- **(c)** A picture of the product doing its thing is proof. It fails as decoration or lifestyle mood.
- **(d)** Every screenshot should show a stone and its object, large, near the moment of reveal. Skip mood art, characters that aren't in the game, and frames that are mostly UI chrome. Use one short caption line that names what the picture proves.

## 5. Where Hopkins clashes with the App Store or a calm brand

1. **Hard-sell length vs. fixed tiny fields.** His readers had chosen to read a magazine page, and his rule was to use all the space. The App Store gives 30 + 170 characters, three visible lines and swiped screenshots. His own concession (chewing gum: "One sentence may tell a complete story") is the bridge, so use completeness, not length.
2. **"Free" as reader-multiplier vs. a free-to-play stigma.** He said "Free" multiplied readers, yet found it cheapened anything serious (Pepsodent: results divided by four). On the App Store every game shows GET, and "free" signals ads and purchases. [U] Apple's metadata guideline (2.3.7, as I recall) also discourages prices in names, subtitles and screenshots. Lead with the risk removers instead.
3. **Urgency and limited offers vs. calm.** He used deadlines ("expires on a certain date", "good only for one week", SA 19) and scarcity ("They will never be offered again", ML IV). False urgency breaks a calm brand and risks review. There is no honest deadline in a free offline game, so drop this lever entirely.
4. **"No humour, no fine writing" vs. a game that is amusement.** He exempts amusement advertising himself (ML XVII). The useful part is the ban on *style for its own sake*. Lines that are lovely but carry no information should earn their place by test.
5. **Boasting.** He condemns boasts (ML IX, XI), yet A1 is a sales boast. On the App Store, superlatives ("the best puzzle game") are both weak (SA 7) and risky in review. Strata's copy has none, which is right.
6. **Scientific certainty vs. small samples.** His laws came from millions of coupons. A small indie app's tests will be noisy, so treat his principles as hypotheses to test, not laws. He himself says "we must feel our way" (ML XIV).
7. **"Tell the full story in every ad" vs. restraint.** A calm page that says everything risks feeling like a pitch. Put the complete case in the first three lines and leave the rest quiet.
8. **Negatives as reassurance.** "No timers. No ads." are negations, which his positive rule would dislike. But his own "no opiates" line shows that negatives work as risk removal after the appeal. Keep them, and keep them last.
9. **Ethics.** The same mechanisms (specific claims, free trial, guarantee, a personality) sold Liquozone's germ cures (A4). In his system, specificity carries whatever is claimed, true or not. For Strata, make only claims you can verify: one solution per stone, offline, no tracking.
10. **Dealer and merchandising schemes.** His secret gifts, dealer naming and coupon redemption have no App Store equivalent beyond featuring and Custom Product Pages. They don't transfer.
11. **Personality.** "Make a man famous" suits an indie maker well, but only with a real person speaking. An invented mascot narrator would be the "frivolous coined name" he warns against (SA 20).
